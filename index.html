Travel with José Rizal to Belgium

Brussels and Ghent, 1890 to 1891. Short on money, heavy with news from home, Rizal spent these months finishing the novel that would shake the colony: El Filibusterismo. Pack your bags and follow him.

Panel 1 Welcome to Belgium

A small kingdomwith a free press

Belgium sits between France, the Netherlands, Germany and Luxembourg, with a short coast on the North Sea.

It became independent in 1830, and its 1831 constitution was one of the most liberal in Europe, protecting freedom of the press. When Rizal arrived, King Leopold II was on the throne. Brussels was the elegant capital, while Ghent in Flanders was a historic university and industrial city.

Panel 2 Travel diary

Walk beside Rizal

Keep scrolling to move from one diary page to the next, or tap a stop on the route.

Stop 01Leaving Paris
28 January 1890
01

"Paris is too costly and too noisy. I need quiet to write."

Why Rizal chose Brussels

  • Living costs in Paris had soared during the Universal Exposition.
  • The city's busy social life kept pulling him away from his writing.
  • He wrote to his friend Ferdinand Blumentritt that he had arrived in "the beautiful city of Brussels."
Why it matters: He gave up comfort for focus. His work came first.
Stop 02A room in Brussels
38 Rue Philippe de Champagne
02

"A modest boarding house, a desk, and my second novel."

Writing day and night

  • Lodged in a boarding house run by the Jacoby sisters.
  • Wrote and revised El Filibusterismo, the sequel to the Noli.
  • Sent articles to La Solidaridad, the newspaper of the Filipino reform movement.
  • Shared lodgings with fellow Filipino José Alejandrino, an engineering student.
Why it matters: Brussels became his writing room, far from the noise of Paris.
Stop 03Bad news from home
1890
03

"Letters from Calamba bring nothing but sorrow."

Calamba in crisis

  • Letters from his brother Paciano told of the worsening land dispute in Calamba.
  • The Dominican hacienda took the Rizal family and other tenants to court to evict them.
  • Restless and worried, he wanted to go home, but friends warned him it was too dangerous.
  • In early August 1890 he left for Madrid to seek justice for his family, without success.
Why it matters: His family's suffering made his second novel darker and angrier.
Stop 04Back to Brussels
April 1891
04

"The manuscript is finished. Now it must be printed."

A finished novel, an empty wallet

  • He finished the manuscript of the Fili in Biarritz, France, on 29 March 1891.
  • Returned to Brussels around mid-April 1891 and stayed again with the Jacobys.
  • Disappointed with quarrels among the reformers in Madrid, he stepped back from the movement to focus on his book.
Why it matters: He chose to fight with a book rather than in political rivalries.
Stop 05Ghent
5 July 1891
05

"Ghent is cheaper. Tea and biscuits will have to do."

Living on almost nothing

  • Moved to Ghent, a university city, because printing there cost less than in Brussels.
  • Found the printer F. Meyer-Van Loo on Vlaanderenstraat, who agreed to print in installments.
  • Pawned his jewels and lived on tea and biscuits to save money.
  • When the funds ran out, printing stopped, and he even thought of burning the manuscript.
Why it matters: The Fili was born out of real sacrifice.
Stop 06The Fili is born
18 September 1891
06

"At last! The first copies are off the press."

A friend saves the book

  • Valentin Ventura in Paris heard of his trouble and sent the money to finish printing.
  • El Filibusterismo came off the press on 18 September 1891.
  • He sent the first copies to José Basa and Sixto Lopez in Hong Kong.
  • The novel was dedicated to Fathers Gomez, Burgos and Zamora (Gomburza), executed in 1872.
Why it matters: Belgium is where Rizal's most daring novel finally reached the world.
Panel 3 Important places

Places Rizal walked

Tap a pin on the map, or choose a place, to see what happened there.

38 Rue Philippe de Champagne

The Jacoby boarding house

Rizal's home in Brussels from early 1890, and again when he returned in 1891. Here he wrote much of El Filibusterismo, read letters from Calamba, and sent articles to La Solidaridad. Brussels city records show him registered at this address on 28 March 1890.

Ghent, Flanders

A famous university city

Rizal moved here on 5 July 1891 because printing was cheaper. He lived very simply with José Alejandrino, counting every coin while the novel was printed page by page.

Vlaanderenstraat, Ghent

F. Meyer-Van Loo Press

The print shop that produced the first edition of El Filibusterismo in 1891. It agreed to print in installments, which suited Rizal's thin purse. The book's title page still names this Ghent printer.

Panel 4 Important people

Faces along the way

The friends, hosts, and helpers who made Rizal's time in Belgium possible.

JS
Hosts in Brussels

The Jacoby sisters

Ran the boarding house where Rizal stayed. A young woman of the house, remembered as "Petite Suzanne," grew fond of him and cried when he left.

JA
Roommate

José Alejandrino

A Filipino engineering student who shared Rizal's rooms. In Ghent he helped find a printer and delivered proofs to the press.

VV
Rescuer, from Paris

Valentin Ventura

When printing stopped for lack of money, this loyal friend sent the funds to finish it. Without him, the Fili might never have appeared.

PR
By letter, from home

Paciano Rizal

His older brother, whose letters about the Calamba eviction case weighed on Rizal throughout his stay.

FB
By letter, Austria

Ferdinand Blumentritt

Rizal's closest European friend and constant correspondent, to whom he described his arrival in Brussels.

JB
First readers, Hong Kong

José Basa & Sixto Lopez

Received the first printed copies of El Filibusterismo, sent by Rizal on the day it came off the press.

He came to Belgium with an unfinished manuscript and empty pockets. He left with a novel that would stir a nation.

Panel 5 Events and observations

What shaped his ideas

How the events of 1890 and 1891 found their way into his thinking and his writing.

01

Injustice at Calamba

The eviction of his family and neighbors by the hacienda showed him how powerless Filipinos were under the colonial system, even in court.

→ The Fili's bitter, angry tone
02

Reform that went nowhere

His failed trip to Madrid and the quarrels among Filipino reformers made him doubt that Spain would ever grant peaceful change.

→ A darker sequel to the Noli
03

Poverty and sacrifice

Living on tea and biscuits and pawning his jewels taught him what it costs to serve one's country with no wealth behind you.

→ Resolve to publish no matter what
04

The memory of Gomburza

He dedicated the novel to the three priests executed in 1872, linking his story to a wound every Filipino remembered.

→ A book written as a tribute and a warning
05

A country with a free press

Belgium's liberal laws allowed books critical of rulers to be printed. In Ghent, Rizal could publish what would have been banned at home.

→ The Fili printed in full

What we can learn

Lessons from Rizal's journey

i.

Persevere when it's hard

Broke and worried, Rizal still finished his book. Great work often happens in the hardest seasons.

ii.

True friends matter

Ventura's help and Alejandrino's support remind us that no one serves the nation alone.

iii.

Turn pain into purpose

Rizal took his family's suffering and gave it a voice that spoke for every Filipino.
